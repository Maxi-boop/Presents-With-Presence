What are Solutions?

A solution is a homogeneous mixure made up of a solute and a solvent. What are some of it's defining properties?

  • Homogeneous mixure
  • Clear and transparent
  • One phase of matter (solid, liquid, gas)
  • Solute doesn't seperate from solvent while standing (when the mixure is not being touched)
  • Filtration doesn't seperate the compounds (non-seperable/reversable)
Wait...what are solvents and solutes?
  • A solvent is a substance dissolving a solute. A common example, is H2O.
  • A solute is a substance BEING dissolved by the solvent. A common example is NaCl.

Starting Formulas:

Formulas play as a key element into understanding how solutions work. As we go on with this unit, these key formulas will be frequently used.

  • Specific Gravity: Density Substance / Denisty H2O
  • Percent Composition by Mass: mass solute / mass solution * 100%
  • Percent Volume: volume solute / volume solution * 100%
  • Parts Per Million (PPM):mass solute / mass solution * 10^6
  • Mole Fraction: moles A / total moles solution
  • *Molarity (M): mol solute / volume in LITERS of Solution
  • *Molality (m): mol solute / KILOGRAMS solvent
  • *: signifies an important formula

**NOTE: solution = solute + solvent.**

Getting into solutions.

Now since you know the basics of solutions, let's figure out how we can use them. To measure a solution we use a tool called a volumetric flask, as you can see in the image below. We use volumetric flasks to precisely measure out X mL of solution.

Next we have a Stock Solution. A stock solution is a solution with a high concentration of solute. The formula for calculating how much stock solution we need to make X mL of solution is as follows:

  • M1V1 = M2V2
    • M1: Molarity of stock solution
    • V1: Volume of stock solution
    • M2: Molarity of dialuted solution
    • V2: Volume of dialuted solution
    Lets try this problem:

    Ans: 69.44mL

    These problems may ask you to even find the molarity of a solution, so don't panic! Remember: M = moles solute / Liters of solution

    Henry's Law

    Henry's law believes that solubility and pressure are proportional. Why is that? Well, since we know that temperature and pressure are also proportional (P/T = P/T), therefore solubility and temperature are proportional. Here's the formula for Henry's law:

  • S1/P1 = S2/P2

    • Concentration

    Concentration is the measure of how much solute is in a solvent. The more solute there is, the more concetrated the solution is.

    As you can see in the image above the more concentrated solution is more amber, while the more diluted solution is more yellow. To define concentration we use the words: unsaturated, saturated, and super-saturated.

    • Unsaturated: There's more solvent than solute.
    • Saturated: Solvent cannot hold more solute. Dissolvement and undissolvement occur at an equal rate.
    • Super-saturated: Solvent is holding more solute than it can handle. Common chemicals can become super-saturated:
      • Sodium Acetate (NaC2H3O2)
      • Sodium Thiosulfate (Na2S2O3)
    • Note: when you add a bit more solute to a super-saturated solution, the entire solution will collapse. When you add a bit more solute to a saturated solution it will sink to the bottom. When you add a bit more solute to an unsaturated solution it will be dissolved.

    Sometimes it may become tricky to identify whether a mixure is a solution or a nonsolution. In this section we will identify different forms of mixures that LOOK like solutions, but in reality they aren't.

    Suspensions are LARGE solute particles that will settle unless agitated/stirred. This is not a solution because a solution is a HOMOGENEOUS mixure, which means that the solute is fully dissolved into the solvent. Below is an example of a suspension of sand and water.

    Colloides Dispersions

    A type of suspension but when the particles are larger than those of a solution but smaller than thos of a suspension. An example of this is milk!

    Aqueous Solutions

    Aqueous solutions are solutions where the solvent is water (H2O).

    Solvation: Formation of a solution when a solute and solvent molecules are combined.
    Dissociation: Seperation of ions when an ionic compound is dissolved. A type of solvation. NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)
    Percipitation: Formation of an insoluable compound due to (usually) double replacement. Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2NaI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2NaNO3 (aq)
    Ionization: When IONS are formed from interactions between the solute and the solvent. CH3COOH + H2O → H3O+ + CH3COO-

    Electrolytes

    An electrolyte is a substance when dissolved in water conducts electricity. Example: NaCl -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq). Strong electrolytes conduct a lot of electircity while weak electrolytes conduct less electricity. NaCl is an example of a STRONG electrolyte. (When salt water has electricity flowing through it, it could kill someone.) Nonelectrolytes have NO flow of electricity when mixed with water. C6H12O6 (glucose) is an example of a nonelectrolyte because it is covalently bonded and does not dissacosiate when mixed with water.

    Net Ionic Equations

    Net ionic equations are derived from PERCIPITATION MOLECULAR EQUATIONS. What this means is that there is an insoluable compound, a solid, that is formed. Table F has information on how to notate whether a compound is a solid or aqueous. Now how do we derive net ionic equations?

    1. Derive the original molecular equation. AgNO3(aq)+NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)
    2. Break each molecule apart to it's ions EXCEPT for the insoluable compounnd. This is to derive the complete ionic equation.
    3. Ag+ (aq) + NO3-(aq) + Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s) + Na+(aq) + NO3-(aq)
    4. Finally remove the Spectator Ions, a.k.a the ions that remain aqueous on both sides of the equation (ex: Na+(aq) and NO3- (aq))
    5. Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)

    Now you have the net ionic equation. But what is the point? The point of the net ionic equation is to derive the equation of which the perciptate is produced. Or in simpler terms: Equation for the percipitate. With this equation we can see what molecules are being used, making life simpler for studying solutions.

    Colligative Properties

    Before we get started on this very confusing, and mathematical unit, let's take a real life example. Why do we sprinkle salt on icy roads to make them less icy, but not glucose? Why does water take longer to boil after adding salt? These phenomenons come from collegative properties. Let's answer these questions of life.

    A colligative property is a property determined by the ratio of solute to solvent particles. Here are important key notes of colligative properties:

    1. Lowers the vapor pressure, therefore increases the boiling point. (*Vapor pressure is increased by voltile substances, colligative properties are dependant on non-volitile substances)
    2. Freezing point decreases.
    3. Increase in osmotic pressure (will talk about later).
    *Note: the more solute added the more spread out the solvent is. Now since we know that how do we derive the increase in boiling point and decrease in freezing point? We have formulas to help us! ΔTb = i * Kb * m(solute), and ΔTf = i * Kf * m(solute) Let's explore what each variable means:

    • ΔTb and ΔTf: Change in boiling and freezing point respectivly.
    • i: the # of particles produced by a solute.
    • Kb and Kf: molal-boiling and molal-freezing point constants respectivly.
    • m(solute): Molality of solute.

    Now let's work through the variables we don't know:

    1. To calculate the i value or Van's Hoff factor, we take a molecule let's say NaCl and see how many particles it produces when dissolved in water. (DISSOCIATION REACTION.). NaCl produces 2 particles when dissolved in water: Na and Cl. Now let's take glucose (C6H12O6). We know that glucose is a nonelectrolyte compound and does NOT dissociate in water (please note dissociating is NOT dissolving). Therefore, the i value would equal 1.
      Still confused? Watch this -> OR read here.
    2. The constant values of Kf and Kb are unique based on what the solvent IS. The Kf for water is 1.86 degrees Celcius/molality. The Kb for water is 0.513 degrees Celcius/molality
    3. Molality is a variable we do know, and defined here. But if you need a refresher it is m = mol solute / kilograms solvent.

    Okay...now since we know that we have to work through some tricks! If we want to calculate the temperature of boiling water when salt is added: you have to add 100 degrees celcius to ΔTb. If we want to calculate what temperature the water will freeze after we add salt substract ΔTf from 0 degrees Celcius. This ONLY applies for water since the boiling point is 100 and freezing point is 0. Other substances may have different boiling and freezing points, but the EXACT same proccess just change up 100 and 0 for the respective temperatures in CELCIUS. *TEMP MUST BE IN CELCIUS TO USE ΔTf and ΔTb. IF NOT CONVERT TO CELCIUS.

    Q: Glucose was dissolved in water. If we had 98 grams of glucose, and the freezing point is now -0.465 degrees celcius, how many grams of water were used?

    1. 0.465 = (1)(1.86)(m)
    2. mol glucose = 98g * 1mol/180g
    3. m = 0.54mol / x kg water
    4. 0.465 = (1)(1.86)(0.54/x)
    5. 0.25 = 0.54 / x
    6. x = 0.54 / 0.25
    7. x = 2.16kg
    8. 2.16kg * 1000g = 2160g of Water

    Osmotic Pressure

    Osmosis: the process of which a solvent flows into a solution via a superpermeable membrane.

    The formula of osmotic pressure is:

  • Π=iMRT
    • Π: osmotic pressure in atmospheres.
    • i: van hoff's factor.
    • M: Molarity of solution.
    • R: Ideal gas constant: 0.0821
    • T: Temperature in KELVIN

    Note: the video above does not have the Van's Hoff factor. Please remember it.

    Raoult's Law

    Vapour pressure of a solution is always lower than that of a solvent.

  • Psoln = XsolvPsolv
    • Psoln: Vapour pressure in solution
    • Xsolv: mole fraction of solvent
    • Psolv: vapour pressure of pure solvent.
